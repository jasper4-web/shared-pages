The Bank · v3 · state
Burn the Boats · Agent 6 · Round 3 · Fifty ideas, ten rendered

It is a bank. Fifty candidates,
forty killed in writing, ten built.

Round 1 was too austere. Round 2 heard "fun" and went goofy — arcade cabinets and primary colours, a screen visibly trying to cheer him up. Neither is right, and the answer is not the midpoint. The target is the feeling of value held: weight, worth, permanence, and a little ceremony when you take some out.

The tuning fork is his own note about the Fridge. A fridge door works because it is a real object that already carries emotional weight — you put things on it you are proud of, and everyone who walks past sees them. It earns its metaphor instead of wearing it as a costume. Every one of the ten below had to clear that bar.

The hardest state — use the switch at the top of this page

Every rendition renders in two states: day 41 with 1,530 XP banked, and day one at zero. Day one is the state that decides whether he opens this tab in week one, and "nothing is affordable yet" must read as promise rather than poverty.

The answer I found is the same in all ten, and it is a content decision rather than a visual one: on day one the nearest thing is not a reward — it is his first privilege, which one 85% week buys. Five days, not eleven. So on day one every direction leads with the privilege, and the rewards stand behind it already named, already carrying the conditions he wrote, waiting.

Step 1 and 2 — fifty candidates, forty killed

Every one scored against what this tab must actually carry: a balance that only rises and reads at a glance · two currencies, one of them objects you hold · five rewards with prices, his own conditions, and locked/affordable/claimed states · a claim moment · history · all of it on a 390px phone and still good at zero. Most metaphors die on history or on five different prices.

01 · THE COUNTING HOUSE
Why it clears the bar
It is the only direction that is literally the thing rather than a metaphor for it. A counting house is where value is held and counted out, and the counter's real zones — glass, tray, shelf, bell, spike — map onto the four content types with nothing left over and nothing invented.
The claim moment
Ringing the bell. A claim that makes a sound is worth ten that flash. It is also the only claim in the set where something is handed to him rather than unlocked by him — which is the correct power relationship for a reward he has already paid for.
Day one
The tray is empty and says so plainly, but it says "your first one is five days off" — not "you have none". The dockets all stand on the shelf already carrying his conditions, so the shelf is stocked from the first morning even though nothing is funded.
Risk: it is the most institutional of the ten, and institutions are exactly what round 1 felt like. It stays warm only because of the mahogany, the handwriting on the dockets and the brass — swap those for greys and it collapses straight back into the austere version.

02 · THE BOX ROOM
Why it clears the bar
It removes the transaction entirely. He is not buying a day off — he is being let into the box it is already sitting in. That reframes every locked reward from a thing withheld into a thing reserved, which is the single most important sentence in this tab.
History, solved by the object
Box 06 stands open and empty with its plate still engraved. A claimed reward does not vanish from the wall, it becomes visible evidence — permanently. No other direction gets history this cheaply.
Day one
All five doors present, engraved and sealed. The ring is bare and says so. The copy underneath does the work: "the first key is closer than the first box."
Risk: brass on steel is one bad decision away from the round-1 vault, and the doors are the closest thing in this set to a grid. It earns its place on the two-key idea; if that idea were cut, this direction should be cut with it.

World: a wine cellar and its cellar book
Primitive: long horizontal objects lying in cradles

This is NOT a countdown — nothing here is late, it is laying down.

03 · THE CELLAR
Why it clears the bar
"Laying down" is the only phrase found in three rounds that makes an unaffordable reward sound like good practice rather than failure. You lay a bottle down on purpose. Nobody has ever felt rebuked by a cellar.
The claim moment
Drawing a bottle — a small, deliberate, slightly ceremonial act you only do when the occasion is right. It matches the weight of spending two thousand XP far better than a button that says CLAIM.
Day one
The strongest day one of the ten. A cellar on its first day is supposed to look full and unready — that is what a cellar is — so zero XP reads as correct rather than empty. Every bottle is already in its bin with his condition chalked on it.
Risk: wine carries class associations he may not want, and there is a live question about whether alcohol imagery is right for a man deliberately curbing two vices. That is his call, not mine, and it is the reason this is ranked third rather than first.

04 · THE ENVELOPES
Why it clears the bar
Thickness is the only representation of a balance in the whole set that you understand without reading. A fat envelope and a thin one need no legend, no percentage and no colour coding — and the difference survives being glanced at for half a second.

05 · THE LOGBOOK
Why it clears the bar
It is the only direction where the second currency needs no explanation at all. Everyone understands that a rating is something a person signs off on and that you cannot purchase one. It also makes "XP only rises" a law of the object rather than a rule the app has to enforce.
History, solved by the object
A logbook entry is never erased. The claimed reward stays on the page in a shaded row marked TAKEN — the record and the reward list are the same document.
Day one
An empty logbook is not a sad object; it is a book you are about to fill. Every rating is already written in with its hours, and the endorsements sit above them awaiting a signature — which is the one thing a single good week produces.
Risk: it is the most sober of the ten and the closest to round 1's register. It also borrows authority from aviation that this app has not earned — if it reads as costume rather than structure, it dies exactly the way round 2's arcade did.

Five jars filling separately implies five separate savings pots, which is not how the balance works — one number funds all five. The labels row underneath states the real figures, but this is the one metaphor in the ten that slightly lies, and it should be said out loud rather than discovered later.
Day one
Six empty jars and the line that earns it: "the labels are the part he did first." Writing the conditions is real work he has already done, and the jars display that work before they display a single coin.
Risk: at 390px six jars are 58px wide each, which is why the names are abbreviated above and given in full underneath. That split is a compromise; if the jar row alone had to carry the names it would fail.

Hand-off · Agent 6 · Round 3

Top three, and the one I most regret killing

What this is

renditions/rend-bank-v3.html — fifty candidates written down and visibly culled with a verdict on each, ten rendered, every one carrying all four content types and rendering in two states: day 41 and day one at zero. v1 and v2 are untouched. I did not touch index.html or any other agent's file.

Ranked

1 · 03 The Cellar. It solves the hardest problem in the tab with a single word. "Laying down" is the only phrase found across three rounds that makes an unaffordable reward sound like good practice instead of failure — you lay a bottle down deliberately, and nobody has ever felt rebuked by a cellar. It also has the best day-one state by construction: a cellar on its first day is supposed to look full and unready, so zero XP reads as correct rather than poor. One live question, which is his and not mine: whether alcohol is the right imagery for a man deliberately curbing two vices. If the answer is no, take the mechanic and re-skin it — the phrase survives the move.

2 · 06 The Shadow Board. The best answer to "day one must feel like promise, not poverty" in any round. A painted outline normally accuses you of a missing tool; here it means the place is already yours. At zero XP the board is fully marked out and nothing is blank — it is the only direction where the empty state is arguably better looking than the full one. Its weakness is honest: the metaphor says tools, which fits the privileges perfectly and a restaurant date only loosely.

3 · 02 The Box Room. Ranked here on one idea that no other candidate produced: a safe deposit box needs two keys turned together, which is the most literal picture of an earned-not-bought permission in the whole fifty. It also reframes the entire tab — he is not buying a day off, he is being let into the box it is already sitting in — and it gets history free, because a claimed box stands open on the wall forever with its plate still engraved.

The one I most regret killing

№50 — Exchequer tally sticks. For four hundred years English public finance ran on notched hazel sticks, and the detail that hurts to lose is this: the stick was split lengthwise and each party kept half, so a debt could only be proved when the two halves were matched. That is a perfect physical picture of a privilege — a permission that exists between two parties and cannot be forged. It is also the literal origin of a "stock holder" and of the word "counterfoil".

It died on legibility. Notches on a stick cannot express five different prices at 390px, and the whole thing needs a paragraph of history before it means anything — which is the exact opposite of the Fridge test, where the object explains itself instantly. Runner-up regret: №31, the passport. Visas are permissions granted by someone else and stamped in permanently, which is the privilege mechanic exactly — but a passport holds no balance, and the balance is half of this tab.

The day-one finding — this matters more than the visual choice

Building all ten at zero surfaced something that applies whichever wins, and it is a content decision rather than a design one. At 0 XP and ~118/day, his cheapest reward is eleven days away, and every direction that leads with rewards on day one reads as a countdown to something distant. But his first privilege is one 85% week away — five days. So on day one the correct headline is the privilege, not the reward. Every rendition here does that, and it turns week one from "nothing for eleven days" into "something by Friday."

The corollary: the reward conditions are the day-one asset. He writes those sentences before he has a single XP, so on day one the tab is already full of his own commitments even though it holds no money. Four of the ten say this out loud — "the writing on them is already true", "the labels are the part he did first", "every place is painted, named and spoken for."

Verification

arsenal-lint: clean ✓. Ten phones rendered headlessly and screenshotted at 390×844 in both states, top and scrolled, and I looked at them. No console errors, no failed requests. No horizontal overflow: every phone reports 388/388, and the gallery page itself was tested at a real 390px viewport with overflow-x:hidden disabled so the result could not be masked — 390 of 390.

Four defects were caught by looking rather than by reading the CSS. The worst was structural: my day-one state machine set display for both the hidden and the shown state, and per-rendition descendant selectors like .x .t>span{display:block} out-specified it — so several renditions rendered both states at once, showing "1,530" and "0" stacked. The fix was to make the state machine hide-only, so a shown element keeps whatever display its own rule gives it, and to scope six selectors to direct children. The others: the envelope flap painted over three reward titles ("A full day ott"), and two headers wrapped to three lines from the same span bug.

For the GM

  • History is now a hard requirement and the shipped app cannot do it. claim() sets r.done=1 and the reward disappears from the list — there is no record of what was taken or when. Every one of these ten renders a claimed item; none of them can be built until a claimed[] record with a date exists.
  • Rewards are still one-shot for all 91 days, and S.privileges is still a bare integer with no way to spend one. Both block the two-currency display that every direction here depends on.
  • Day-one copy is the real deliverable. Whichever skin wins, the week-one headline should be the privilege at five days, not the cheapest reward at eleven.
  • Only the Jar Shelf bends the truth — five jars filling separately implies five pots when there is one balance. It is called out on the page rather than hidden. If it wins, that needs resolving.
