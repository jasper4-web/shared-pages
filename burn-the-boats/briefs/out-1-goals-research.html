OUT-1 · GOALS RESEARCH — what the real systems actually do

Agent 1 · Burn the Boats crew 2026-07-25. Companion to out-1-goals-spec.md.

Scope of the question. Not "which framework is best." The question is narrower and harder: what mechanics survive contact with one person, 91 weekdays, ≤60 seconds of daily interaction, and a governing rule that a wall of failure ends the product. Every verdict below is against that test, not against the framework's own claims.

Two things are already implemented and are not up for re-litigation — the spec extends them: 4DX lead/lag, and the 12 Week Year weekly execution score at 85% (GOAL-ENGINE.md).

1 · OKR

Actual mechanics. An Objective is a qualitative ambition; 3–5 Key Results are the measurable evidence it happened. Key Results are outcomes, not tasks. Google grades on a 0.0–1.0 scale and splits goals into committed (expected 1.0, graded pass/fail — 0.63 is a fail) and aspirational / moonshot (0.7 is a triumph). The "sweet spot of ambitious realism" is an average of 0.6–0.7 across the set — you are expected to miss a well-set OKR about 30% of the time (Google re:Work · What Matters — how to grade OKRs).

The documented failure at individual scale. This is the best-evidenced failure in the whole research set and it is directly relevant. Cascading OKRs — company → department → team → person — is widely reported as the thing that kills them. At the individual level OKRs "blur into performance goals rather than collective outcomes"; handed-down goals produce disengagement because the owner had no hand in writing them; and cascading creates a wait state, where a level cannot be set until the level above finalises. Multiple practitioner sources now recommend stopping at three layers and explicitly not cascading to individuals (Weekdone · Caroli.org · Christina Wodtke · PerformSpark).

A second documented failure: quarterly rigidity. Once set, OKRs resist mid-quarter change because change reads as instability — so they degrade into "distant checkboxes rather than living guides" (Xodiac).

What transfers. The O/KR split. In this app the equivalent is already half-built: lag is the Objective, lead is the Key Result. What is missing is that a KR must be measurable — today three of the six December goals (FAITH, MIND, PEOPLE) carry at:30/target:100 unit:'%', a number he types about himself. That is not a key result, it is a mood. The spec replaces every hand-typed at with a derived ledger.

What to reject, and why.

  • Cascading, in the corporate sense. It is worth being precise here, because this app does cascade and must. The failure mode in the literature is authorship: goals arrive from above and the owner never wrote them. Here there is exactly one author, so the disengagement mechanism cannot fire. What does transfer is the wait state — the spec must never let a lower level be un-writable because an upper level is unset. Hence: every level pre-fills and is independently editable; a block never blocks on a milestone.
  • 0.7-as-success grading. Rejected outright. Deliberately planning to land at 70% is indistinguishable from the 85%-execution threshold already in the app except that it moves the goalposts after the fact. Post-hoc regrading is the single most corrosive thing you can do to a scoreboard someone has to believe.
  • Confidence scores (the "I'm at 0.4 on this" mid-cycle check). Rejected for the daily surface, kept as a weekly one: it is a cheap, honest signal on a lag goal that no execution score can give, and it costs one tap. See spec §5, goal.confidence.

2 · The 12 Week Year (Moran & Lennington)

Actual mechanics. Compress the annual planning horizon to 12 weeks so "the week is the new month." Each 12-week year has 12 weekly plans of tactics; each day you tick the tactics you completed; at week's end execution score = tactics completed ÷ tactics due, and the book's number is 85% = a won week. The governing line is "you are scoring your execution, not your results." Scoring is reviewed in a 15–20 minute Weekly Accountability Meeting; the Friday-afternoon self-score variant is the solo form (O'Reilly — 12 Week Year Field Guide, ch.5 "Scorekeeping" · Asian Efficiency · Ian Koniak).

Why 12 weeks and not 12 months — the argument is about procrastination arithmetic, and it is the strongest single argument in the research set for how to space horizons: with 12 months you have 11 months to procrastinate; with 12 weeks you have this week. Falling a month behind in an annual plan produces no urgency to catch up (Pipedrive · Mindtools).

The known failure — and it is this project's failure. The most common practitioner complaint is collapse around week 5–6, when the plan and reality diverge and the scorecard becomes an accusation (Griply — "Why your 12 Week Year collapses by week 6"). That is precisely the wall of failure that killed v1 of Burn the Boats. The 12WY has no redemption mechanic. Its answer to a bad week is a lower number.

What transfers. Everything already implemented — and one thing not: the 12WY explicitly deletes the month as a planning unit. That is a direct input to the horizon question in the spec.

What to reject. The 12-week container itself. Jul 27 → Dec 1 is 18 weeks; carving it into 12+6 would put a planning ceremony at week 12 (≈ Oct 19) for no reason other than fidelity to a book. The run is the container. Rejected.

3 · EOS / Traction (Wickman)

Actual mechanics. The V/TO holds 10-Year Target → 3-Year Picture → 1-Year Plan → Quarterly Rocks. The 1-Year Plan is 3–7 measurable priorities; Rocks are the 3–7 most important things for the next 90 days; Core Values / Core Focus / 10-Year Target "change rarely." Re-planning happens at the quarterly meeting ("operating in a 90-day world") and the annual session. The named discipline is restraint — most teams want 15–20 goals, and "when everything is a priority, nothing is" (EOS Worldwide — V/TO · EOS Worldwide — quarterly Rocks · Ninety.io — Rocks · Provident CPAs).

What transfers — and this is the most underrated finding in the whole set. The EOS ladder is not a tree. The 3-Year Picture is not the sum of three 1-Year Plans; it is an independently-written dated picture of what things look like then. Levels are linked by coherence, not by arithmetic. That matters enormously for the restructuring requirement: if December were the sum of its milestones, editing a milestone would silently rewrite December. Because it is a picture, it does not. The spec adopts this: milestones justify a goal, they do not compute it.

The ≤7 cap also transfers, but relocated. Six domain goals across 18 weeks is fine at the goal layer — BODY, FAITH and PEOPLE are maintenance, not campaigns. The cap belongs one level down, at the week, where capacity is genuinely finite (20 deep blocks Mon–Fri).

What to reject. The 90-day Rock as the only re-planning unit. Eighteen weeks gives two quarterly checkpoints. Two chances to notice a plan is wrong, in a run where "I feel scrambled" is the stated starting condition, is not enough. The owner's own words — "a system instead of a linear path that we wrote down and can't really be changed" — are a direct rejection of the quarterly-lock discipline.

4 · 4DX (McChesney, Covey, Huling)

Actual mechanics. D1: narrow to a Wildly Important Goal — pick the most important, optionally a second, and no more than two, because the point is narrowing. D2: act on lead measures — the high-impact behaviours that are predictive of the lag AND within your control. D3: a compelling scoreboard, whose test is "can I tell at a glance whether I am winning?" D4: a cadence of accountability — a short weekly meeting (20–30 min) where each person reports last week's commitment, reviews the scoreboard, and makes the next commitment (FranklinCovey · Perdoo guide · Mooncamp).

What transfers. Lead/lag is in. Two things are not:

  1. The ≤2 WIG rule, applied honestly. The app currently treats six domains as six co-equal goals. The profile does not: SANO is priority 1, and the December WIG is explicitly "3 SANO clients AND at least one paying full freight." One WIG, one runner-up (trading — it is also the only daily-cash line), and four maintenance goals. The spec encodes rank, and the rank is what the auto-layout uses when it allocates peak blocks.
  2. The commitment, as a first-class object. In 4DX the weekly unit is not a task list, it is "one or two specific commitments that will most affect the scoreboard." Today's app has no commitment object — week().tactics.b1 is a free string with no owner, no size, and no parent. This is the single largest structural gap and the spec's commit object closes it.

What to reject / adapt. D4 assumes a team. Solo, the cadence has to be supplied by the software: the Friday ritual is the WIG meeting, and the app plays the role of the person who asks "what did you commit to last week?" That is why the ritual must open with the carry-over of last week's commitments and not with a blank form.

5 · GTD — Horizons of Focus (Allen)

Actual mechanics. Six altitudes: Runway (actions) · 10k (projects) · 20k (areas of responsibility) · 30k (goals, 1–2 yr) · 40k (vision, 3–5 yr) · 50k (purpose). The load-bearing part is not the ladder, it is the cadence: the Weekly Review reconciles Runway against Projects; 20k areas are reviewed monthly; visions quarterly; purpose and principles annually (GTD — The 6 Horizons of Focus · Capture-GTD tutorial · Smart Remote Gigs summary).

What transfers. One idea, and it is decisive for the spec's §2: each altitude has its own review frequency, and higher altitudes are reviewed less often. Nothing else in the researched set says this explicitly, and everything else quietly assumes every level gets touched every cycle. The direct consequence: the December goals must not appear in the Friday ritual as editable fields. They are a monthly-or-on-demand altitude. Putting them in the weekly flow is how a 60-second ritual becomes a 10-minute one, and how a stable goal starts drifting weekly.

What to reject. Five of the six altitudes. Purpose (50k) and vision (40k) are answered once, in PROFILE.md, and they are already better answered there than any in-app form would manage. 20k "areas of responsibility" is what the six DOMAINS already are. So GTD contributes a cadence rule, not a ladder.

6 · Personal systems (non-corporate), recent

6a · Yearly Themes — CGP Grey / Myke Hurley

A theme ("the Year of Order") replaces a resolution. It is deliberately abstract so it "can endure minor setbacks and adjust to changing circumstances," and — the operative sentence — "there is no failure as long as the new plans fit the theme," in contrast to resolutions' pass/fail mindset (CGP Grey — 2025 Yearly Themes · Geek to Geek · 168 System · The Theme System).

Steal: the existence of an unfailable layer. This is the structural answer to law #1. A system where every layer is scored has nowhere to put a bad fortnight. Burn the Boats already has the parts — "burn the boats," "become the man I want to be" — but they are decoration, not a layer. The spec makes the run's name and one line an actual tier (S.horizon) that is never scored, never red, and is what the app shows him on a week he lost. Reject the pure form: a theme alone never reaches a Tuesday block.

6b · Slow Productivity — Cal Newport (2024)

Three principles (do fewer things · natural pace · obsess over quality), executed through multi-scale planning: a seasonal/quarterly plan of major objectives, a weekly plan that interprets the quarter, and a daily time-blocked plan. The reduction target is explicitly the count of concurrent commitments (Griply summary · Newport's multi-scale planning).

Steal: the weekly plan's job description — interpret the level above, do not re-derive it. This is exactly the owner's "this is the monthly goal, let me tweak it and add these changes." It is why the Friday ritual pre-fills from the nearest milestone plus last week's carry-over, and why a blank weekly form is a failed design.

Reject: "work at a natural pace." He needs income by February–March. The app should not counsel patience it cannot afford.

6c · Four Thousand Weeks — Oliver Burkeman (2021)

A fixed-volume approach: a closed list and a hard cap of three work-in-progress items (from Personal Kanban) — nothing new starts until something finishes; plus "pay yourself first" — the most important work gets the first and best hours of the day, not the residue (Nate Anglin's notes · Sajith Pai summary · APM).

Steal, both:

  • Pay yourself first is already half-built — blocks 1 and 2 (09:00–12:00) are labelled PEAK WINDOW, matching the profile's "peak brain 9 AM–noon, protect it absolutely." Nothing in code enforces that the WIG gets them. The spec's auto-layout does.
  • Fixed volume is the honest form of the capacity problem: 4 blocks × 5 weekdays = 20 deep blocks a week, and that number does not negotiate. Committing to more is the arithmetic origin of a wall of failure. This is where the WIP cap belongs — on active weekly commitments, not on goals.

7 · Four findings from the underlying literature that change the design

These are not frameworks, they are evidence, and each one settles a specific open question in the spec.

7a · If-then plans beat intentions — d ≈ 0.65. Gollwitzer & Sheeran's meta-analysis (94 studies, 8,000+ participants) puts implementation intentions at a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, robust to publication bias; a 2024 meta-analysis over 642 tests finds effects are larger when the plan keeps a genuine contingent if-then form and the person is highly motivated (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, PDF · Goals & Progress summary · The when and how of planning, 2024).

→ Consequence. A weekly commitment must not be stored as "outreach conversations." It must be stored with when — a specific block on a specific day. commit.blocks and week.grid exist because of this finding, not because a grid looks tidy. The cascade is an if-then plan generator.

7b · The what-the-hell effect. After one lapse, all-or-nothing thinkers treat the slip as total failure and abandon the goal; the slip is rarely the problem, the response to it is. Aiming at perfection makes quitting more likely after a single miss. The "never miss twice" rule is the standard mitigation (Psychology Today · NCBI · Cohorty — psychology of streaks).

→ Consequence. The owner's escalating-penance mechanic is not a soft option — it is the literature-supported design, and the 12WY/OKR alternative (a lower score, a wiped streak) is the documented abandonment path. This also settles the restructuring question: changing a goal must never zero a counter, because a zeroed counter is a manufactured lapse.

7c · Temporal landmarks genuinely motivate. Dai, Milkman & Riis (Management Science, 2014) find gym visits, diet searches and goal commitments spike after landmarks — the start of a week, month, semester, a birthday — because landmarks open a new mental accounting period that relegates past imperfection to a prior self; the follow-up shows the effect is causal (Wharton PDF · Management Science · 2015 follow-up PDF).

→ Consequence. This is the tiebreaker on horizon spacing. Milestone dates should be free (his "get ready enough that I feel I could take a class" does not land on the 30th) but the app should default-propose month boundaries, because a landmark date is worth real motivational points that an arbitrary Tuesday is not. Default, never schema. It also argues for the Monday-morning and month-turn moments to be the app's loudest re-engagement pushes.

7d · Performance goals hurt on novel, complex tasks. Locke & Latham: on a complex or genuinely new task, a specific performance-outcome goal produces tunnel vision and can interfere with acquiring the knowledge needed to do the task at all — the documented air-traffic-control simulation result. What is needed there is a learning goal (work out and document the method), not more motivation (Locke & Latham 2006, PDF · Stanford retrospective PDF).

→ Consequence, and it is specific. "3 SANO clients" is a performance goal on a task he has never completed — he has signed zero clients. The profile says he is not blocked on assets, he is blocked on process clarity, and feels "scrambled"; the onboarding process is "crowded and muddy." That is the literature's exact signature of a case needing a learning goal. So the goal model needs a kind field: 'outcome' | 'learning' | 'habit', and the SANO December goal should carry at least one learning milestone ("the delivery process is written down and I have signed off on it") ahead of the outcome milestones. The existing six-item Anthony gate is already that milestone in disguise — the spec formalises it rather than inventing anything.

8 · What this leaves the spec

Consolidated, the steal list is:

  1. Lead/lag (4DX) — in already. Keep.
  2. Execution = done ÷ due, 85% (12WY) — in already. Keep, and do not add a second denominator.
  3. Objective ≠ Key Result (OKR) — split the qualitative goal from the measurable evidence, and make the evidence derived, never typed.
  4. Dated pictures, not a computed tree (EOS) — milestones justify a goal; they never compute it.
  5. Rank, and ≤2 WIGs (4DX) — encode priority; the WIG gets the 09:00–12:00 peak blocks.
  6. The commitment as a first-class weekly object (4DX / 12WY) — the largest gap in the current build.
  7. Per-altitude review cadence (GTD) — December goals do NOT appear in the weekly ritual.
  8. An unfailable top layer (Yearly Themes) — what the app shows on a week he lost.
  9. The week interprets, it does not re-derive (Newport) — the Friday form is never blank.
  10. Fixed volume: 20 deep blocks/week, and pay yourself first (Burkeman) — the capacity truth.
  11. If-then, with a when (Gollwitzer) — a commitment without a block is not a plan.
  12. Never zero a counter on a change (what-the-hell effect) — the restructuring safety rule.
  13. Landmark dates as defaults (fresh-start effect) — free dates, landmark suggestions.
  14. A learning goal ahead of the outcome goal on SANO (Locke & Latham) — the process clarity he named.

The reject list is: cascading-as-authorship, 0.7 grading, the 12-week container, the quarterly-only re-plan, five of six GTD altitudes, theme-only systems, and "work at a natural pace."
